\textbf{Project Description:} \textcolor{red}{250 words}

\vspace*{0.5\baselineskip}

Sampling and exploring large parameter spaces using LANL's production
codes often fails due to robustness issues. Machine learning
techniques are significantly affected by the robustness issue as they
require numerous simulations to learn the functional dependence on
input parameters.  The failure probabibilty is high when the
models/approximation techniques involve various time/space scales,
multi-rate time stepping, adaptive multi-resolution, and/or mix
approximation paradigms. We posit that robustness can be achieved by ensuring that every piece of
the computational chain maintains physical bounds.
\vspace*{0.5\baselineskip}

One key tool to maintain physical bounds is limiting, but most known
limiting techniques are specialized and intrusive.  They mostly
consist of limiting slopes or fluxes, and are all somewhat based on
the flux-corrected transport paradigm of
\cite{Boris_Book_JCP_1973} and
\cite{Zalesak_1979} by which a “non-admissible” but accurate
high-order solution is squeezed in the direction of a “good” low-order
solution until it meets required bounds.  The squeezing idea is
powerful, but has four major defects: (i) It is intrusive: i.e., it
requires manipulating slopes and fluxes which is impossible to do in
LANL's production codes without engaging in costly and error-prone
restructuration; (ii) It fails when the high-order and low-order
solutions do not carry the same “generalized” mass; (iii) It cannot be
applied to modal approximation techniques and reduced-order models;
(iv) It fails for heterogeneous time/space approximations.
\vspace*{0.5\baselineskip}

The goal of this FS is to develop generic limiting techniques that are
not affected by the above problems and that are robust, efficient, HPC
portable, and potentially easy to implement in LANL's production
codes.

\textbf{Problem, Hypothesis \& Technical Rationale:}
\vspace*{0.5\baselineskip}

\textit{Why:} Accurate quantitative predictions of coupled multiphysics  systems
are at the center of LANL's mission and many innovative applications like
hypersonic flight, high energy density phenomena, control and
manipulation of high-speed flows involving complex fluids, mixtures,
and explosives, possibly interacting with radiation and
electromagnetic fields. Some of these
applications have the potential to enable new energy sources by
forming and controlling plasmas with magnetic confinement (tokamaks and
stellarators) or inertial confinement (ICF).  But simulating complex multiphysics
problems involving multiple time and length scales \textbf{still poses}
robustness challenges both for direct and inverse
methods.

\vspace*{0.5\baselineskip}

\textit{Why now:} Although the machine learning revolution promises to solve some of
these problems, data sampling is a major bottleneck due to \textbf{lack of
robustness} for many sample-driven computational approaches such as
surrogate-based optimization, Bayesian inverse problems, and
uncertainty quantification.  Machine learning requires running production
codes many times in order to learn the functional
dependence with respect to input parameters. But sampling large
parameter spaces via thousands of automated runs of complex
multiphysics codes using traditional approximation methods is
hindered by the fact that a significant number of simulations never
reach completion due to robusness issues, thereby undermining the
promises of the machine learning revolution.  There is therefore an
\textbf{urgent need} for the development of agile, non-intrusive, and provably
robust numerical methods that can reliably solve multiphysics
applications using heterogeneous approximation techniques.

\vspace*{0.5\baselineskip}

\textit{What:} We posit that a key to achieving robustness when leveraging
heterogeneous approximation techniques is to ensure that every piece
of the computational chain maintains physical bounds (\ie is
invariant-domain preserving (IDP)).
The objective of this FS is to
address the robustness issue induced by the violation of physical
bounds in the context of heterogeneous approximation techniques.
In this FS we will develop generic limiting techniques that are
robust, efficient, HPC portable, and potentially easy to implement in
LANL's production codes.

\vspace*{0.5\baselineskip}

\textit{How:} Enforcing physical bounds is usualy done in the
literature by limiting slopes or fluxes. Most limiting methods consist
of squeezing some accurate (but fragile) approximation towards a
robust (but inaccurate) approximation until some physical bounds are
met. This powerful methodology is unfortunately highly intrusive as it
needs to access and manipulate fluxes and blend high-order and
low-order fluxes. The intrusiveness renders this methodology
inapplicable for LANL's codes. It is also extremely cumbersome when
deployed on multiphysics problems as the fluxes in this cases may not
be easily identified, especially when they are estimated in a
matrix-free fashion, which is now the accepted standard at LANL for
GPUs accelerated simulations.
%
The new idea to be deployed in the context of this FS is based on an
algorithm recently proposed by \cite{Guermond_arxiv_2026} and based on
\cite[\S2.3]{Kucharik_Shashkov_Wendroff_JCP_2003} and
\citet{Liu_Cheng_Shu_JSC_2017,Griffin_Shu_RMathSci_2026}. This is a
post-processing method informed of the physics through some a priori
reasonable mathematical knowledge of the bounds that are expected.
The input for the proposed methods is a list of (physically relevant) states,
and a user-defined concave functional encoding some a priori
mathematical knowledge on the local bounds that must be enforced. The
main idea is to locally take from the states that are in bounds and
give to the states that are out of bounds while maintaining local
conservation. The idea sounds simple, but the two main difficulties
that make it non-trivial are that the states and the budgets are
vector-valued and the bounds are nonlinear. The method has
already been demonstrated to be efficient and versatile, and it is
indeed non-intrusive.

\textbf{Approach, Resources \& Path Forward:}

\vspace*{0.5\baselineskip}

\textit{How:} To some extent, the proposed methodology will be agnostic to
the problem and discretization at hand. It will consist of a
post-processing algorithm that, if needed, will slightly reorganize the
inputs in a conservative way so that the output satisfies pre-assigned
physical bounds. The input will be a list of physical states (\ie
$\Real^m$-valued state vectors where $m$ is the dimension of the state
variables) say $(\bsfu_i)_{i\in\calV}$, a user-defined quasi-concave
(or simply concave) functional $\Psi$ encoding some a priori
mathematical knowledge on the physical bounds to be enforced, a
list of minimum bounds say $(\Psi_i^{\min})_{i\in\calV}$ to be
enforced. To fully formulate the problem we assume to be given a list
of local masses $(m_i)_{i\in\calV}$ (scaling like volumes). With
$I\eqq \text{card}(\calV)$, the objective is to construct a mapping
$\calL : (\Real^m)^I \to (\Real^m)^I$ so that $\Psi(\calL(\bsfu)_i)\ge
\Psi_m^{\min}$ for all $i\in\calV$. This must be done while being
conservative, meaning $\sum_{i\in\calV} m_i\calL(\bsfu)_i =
\sum_{i\in\calV} m_i\bsfu_i$. The principles of the method are
described in \citep{Guermond_arxiv_2026}.

\vspace*{0.5\baselineskip}

\textit{Deliverable:} The deliverable will be a standalone software
written in a modern object-oriented language (C++ or F2023).
